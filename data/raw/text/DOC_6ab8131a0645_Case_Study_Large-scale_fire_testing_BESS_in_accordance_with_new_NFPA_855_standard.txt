Contributed by Mike Watson | Head of Product, North America with Trina Storage
At the beginning of the year, the National Fire Protection Association’s (NFPA) new Standard for the Installation of Stationary Energy Storage Systems (NFPA 855) went into effect. The updated NFPA 855 now mandates Large-Scale Fire Testing (LSFT) for Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) to assess how fire propagation and heat release perform in real-world, multi-unit installations and to verify that a unit’s thermal runaway won’t ignite adjacent units.
Additionally, NFPA 855 now requires that a Registered Design Professional (RDP) review the LSFT. An RDP is a company or entity that has experience with the testing, may sit on one or more of the Standard Technical Panels (STP), and is a registered Professional Engineer (PE).
As Head of Product North America at Trina Storage, a global leader in energy storage, I recently participated in an LSFT of a full-scale 5 megawatt-hour (MWh) Elementa 2 Pro Platform. This case study shares my experience and offers insights into how to safely and successfully conduct a fire test of this magnitude.
Setting Up a Real-Site Layout for Large-Scale Fire Testing
TÜV Rheinland (TÜV), a Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory (NRTL), conducted the test in November 2025. For the RDP, Trina Storage specifically chose to work with The Hiller Companies (Hiller), a leading North American fire-protection and life-safety engineering firm. Hiller has an outstanding reputation and is engaged with authorities having jurisdiction (AHJs), utilities, and insurers. Trina Storage executed the test in accordance with UL 9540A:2025-11, with results intended to support NFPA 855:2026-based system design, permitting, and risk evaluation under conditions more stringent than minimum industry test requirements.
The LSFT’s purpose is to replicate a thermal runaway event in a utility-scale BESS, quantify the resulting thermal runaway exposure, evaluate system response, and determine whether thermal runaway propagates to adjacent units or assemblies. This allows us to validate safe system design and installation practices.
We carried out the LSFT on a full-scale 1:1 site layout with multiple testing conditions deliberately escalated and thermal challenges amplified beyond standard requirements:
● Cabinet spacing of 10 cm (<4 in): Heat concentration intensified
Containers were arranged back-to-back and side-to-side with only 10 cm of separation, far closer than the 60 cm standard distance, minimizing heat dissipation and increasing thermal stress. This stringent spacing setup allowed us to stress-test container deformation that could affect adjacent containers.
● Maximum energy configuration: 100% State of Charge (SOC) installation
The initiating cabinet was fully populated and charged to 100% SOC, maximizing energy density. Battery modules at the top of adjacent containers were also fully charged, significantly increasing the likelihood of propagation.
● Fire suppression disabled: Safety design thoroughly tested without intervention
All automatic suppression and active extinguishing functions remained disconnected throughout the test, requiring the system to rely solely on its structural composition and engineering-level safety design.
● Pressure-relief panels and the ventilation system: Oxygen flow boosted
The ventilation system and all pressure-relief and deflagration panels remained open to intensify oxygen flow. This setup increased combustion strength and thermal load beyond the limits of real-world failure scenarios.
● Extensive sensors and capture devices: Quantifying the test
Since the process involves several variables, data from the LSFT was captured with thermocouples, heat flux sensors, video cameras, thermal cameras, air speed sensors, oxygen level sensors, a collection setup, and voltage sensors, which allowed for a holistic view of how the thermal runaway affected all parts of the site layout.
One potential issue that may arise during any LSFT test is the inability to initiate a thermal runaway. One safety-related property of lithium-ion technology is its high threshold for thermal runaway compared to other chemistries. Sounds like a good thing, right? Actually, since the purpose of UL 9540A testing is to assess how fire affects adjacent units and assemblies, thermal runaway must be induced.
With this in mind, we prepared a primary, secondary, and tertiary ignition module. First, we equipped a primary module with film heaters strategically placed between four individual battery cells within a module. We then prepared a second module in the same manner, just in case the primary failed to induce thermal runway. Finally, we had a third module exposed to a flamethrower as a last resort scenario.
The Results of the Large-Scale Fire Test
I was fortunate to be the one who pushed the button that initiated the test. The internal film heaters were triggered and began ramping up at a rate of approximately 5°C per minute. After about an hour of increasing heat, flames began pouring from the roof of the initiating container — a good sign. The flames then quickly spread across the entire top of the unit, as expected, and we could smell the burning heavy metal (don’t worry, we were all masked).
During the test, the igniting container experienced sustained flames exceeding 1300°C, simulating an extreme fire scenario. Although massive flames spread from the initiating container to the top of the target backside container, cell temperatures in the adjacent containers remained far below critical safety limits. The maximum cell temperature of the back-to-back container reached 43.9°C, the side-to-side container below 23.9°C, and the face-to-face container below 28.6°C. The visible fire lasted a total of nine hours, but the container remained too hot to open for another three full days.
Following the test, a detailed inspection showed that some components in the electrical compartment of the initiating container were not only intact but also operational.
Adjacent containers remained structurally sound and fully functional. Fire protection systems in adjacent containers maintained 100% availability, module-level thermal management systems remained operational, and cell charge/discharge performance was unaffected.
When we checked the back-side target container, internal components remained unscathed, and the batteries were performing as expected. The flame that spread across the top of the backside target container caused only superficial damage, with scorch marks and burnt paint. If the fire had spread internally, there would have been a larger problem, and the batteries could have potentially succumbed to excessive heating.
Hiller had an engineering representative on-site throughout the entire process, who provided thoughtful recommendations and insightful feedback and was integral to our shared success with the LSFT. TÜV Rheinland completed the formal UL 9540A documentation for our LSFT and officially presented the full test report at the World Future Energy Summit.
Despite the extreme testing conditions of an LSFT, Elementa 2 Pro demonstrated effective system-level safety design by maintaining structural stability and controlled thermal behavior, with no observed thermal runaway propagation to adjacent containers. By exceeding standard acceptance criteria, the results confirm Elementa 2 Pro’s strong system-level reliability and robust design under severe fire conditions. This test serves as an industry benchmark for the updated edition of NFPA 855 and validates Elementa 2 Pro’s safety design features, enabling permitting and regulatory approval.
