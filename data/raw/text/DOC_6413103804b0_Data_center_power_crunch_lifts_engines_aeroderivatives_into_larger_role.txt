A wave of procurement announcements over the past two weeks has underscored how quickly reciprocating engines and aeroderivative turbines are moving from backup duty to primary power for hyperscale data centers, as developers work around multi-year grid interconnection queues.
Wärtsilä said Wednesday it had booked an order for 412 MW of engine capacity to support a hyperscale data center project in Ohio, comprising 40 of its 34SG spark-ignited gas engines. The order marks the first deployment of the 34SG model in a data center application and pushes Wärtsilä’s total U.S. data center engine capacity past 1.6 GW across four projects. Equipment deliveries are scheduled to align with a commercial operation date in early 2028.
The Ohio plant will run as primary power, Wärtsilä said, allowing the site to operate off-grid and avoid interconnection delays that have become routine across PJM and other U.S. energy markets.
“As AI and cloud demand accelerate, operators need energy solutions that are efficient, resilient, and available on their timeline, not the grid’s,” said Risto Paldanius, vice president, Americas at Wärtsilä.
The same day, Missouri-based PROENERGY announced a contract to supply Crusoe with 13 PE6000 aeroderivative gas turbine generator sets, each rated at 50 MW, for upcoming hyperscale AI data center projects. The aggregate 650 MW package is being test-fit at PROENERGY’s Sedalia plant before shipment, a practice the company uses to compress site commissioning schedules.
The procurement activity is not confined to single-project deals. INNIO Group and Rehlko signed a framework agreement earlier this month securing 1.25 GW of Jenbacher gas engine capacity over three years, expanding Rehlko’s earlier 700 MW reservation. Rehlko will deliver the projects through Clarke Energy, its engineering and lifecycle services arm, which has a more than 30-year relationship with INNIO and supports a global installed base exceeding 10 GW.
“Securing multi-year supply enhances visibility and confidence in delivery at a time when demand is being driven by structural, rather than cyclical, market forces,” said Brian Melka, Rehlko’s chief executive.
The announcements extend a pattern visible across the sector. In March, developer Nscale said it would use Caterpillar G3500 reciprocating engines to support 2 GW of onsite generation at its Monarch Compute Campus in Mason County, West Virginia, a project tied to Microsoft and NVIDIA under a letter of intent for up to 1.35 GW of AI compute capacity. That site is being designed to operate independently of the local grid, with a future interconnection preserved as an option.
The underlying driver is a capacity bottleneck on the interconnection side. Grid-power capacity for existing data center projects is largely committed through 2030 in most U.S. markets, according to CBRE’s latest North American Data Center Trend Report, and total capacity under construction in the eight primary markets declined in 2025 for the first time since 2020 as permitting, zoning and power procurement delays slowed the pipeline.
Research firm Cleanview earlier this year identified 46 U.S. data center projects representing a combined 56 GW of planned behind-the-meter capacity, roughly 30% of tracked planned capacity. Ninety percent of those projects were announced in 2025.
The load itself continues to grow faster than forecasters anticipated a year ago. EPRI now projects data centers could account for 9% to 17% of U.S. electricity consumption by 2030, up from 4% to 5% today, with the new estimate roughly 60% higher than the institute’s 2024 projection.
The shift has translated into order book visibility that extends well beyond traditional industrial cycles. Smaller gas turbines and reciprocating engines are increasingly filling roles once reserved for large frame units, which face multi-year lead times and constrained manufacturing slots.
