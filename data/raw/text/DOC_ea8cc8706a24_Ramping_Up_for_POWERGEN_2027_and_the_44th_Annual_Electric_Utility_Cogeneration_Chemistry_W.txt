Where Will the Electrons Come From?
Regarding short-term power needs, the elephant in the room is the growing electrical demand for data centers, which are proliferating throughout the country. Numerous reports suggest that gas-fired units will supply much of this power, in part related to current political headwinds towards renewables development. With time of the essence, simple cycle plants may be a common choice. From an efficiency standpoint, combined cycle power plants are preferrable, and indeed a number of newly announced projects will be of the combined cycle variety.1 Complicating factors are supply and demand issues and moving equipment fabrication through the pipeline.2 Another key component facing backlog issues are transformers.
Supporting efficiency efforts is continued development of cogeneration and combined heat & power (CHP) plants that extract much more energy from steam than conventional fossil-fired units.3 Critical at these facilities are proper makeup water treatment and steam generation chemistry control; topics that this author has addressed in previous Power Engineering articles, and which can be found on the PE website. Intriguing will be to what extent hydrogen becomes a partial or sole fuel for combustion turbines. Many arguments for and against hydrogen production viability have appeared in recent years, and the topic will definitely be on the list for POWERGEN 2027.
Per reports in this publication and others, the U.S. may be moving towards a nuclear renaissance. Small modular reactors (SMRs) appear to be a leading technology, and many people have pointed out that the U.S. Navy has successfully used small reactors for decades. SMRs and other reactor types will likely take longer to permit and build than the gas turbine technologies listed above, but they could become a solid core of future energy production. Like combined cycle and cogeneration units, diligent chemistry control, including cooling water treatment, will be extremely important.
Another energy source that is heating up (pardon the pun) is geothermal. Using the earth’s natural heat is logical, but installing the underground infrastructure is not necessarily a simple task. Researchers remain undaunted, and geothermal energy production should continue to grow. Per an EUC2W perspective, issues related to steam purity and optimizing heat transfer will be very important. Perhaps some geothermal locations will be good spots for cogeneration facilities.
Other Water Treatment and Back End Issues
Issues related to power/industrial plant water makeup, conservation and discharge treatment remain highly important. Let’s briefly examine some of these.
By mandate or sometimes by choice, an increasing number of power and industrial plants are selecting municipal wastewater treatment (referred hereafter as POTW) effluent as the makeup supply to help conserve fresh water sources. For the many POTW plants that have only primary and secondary treatment, the effluent contains organics, phosphate, ammonia, nitrite/nitrate, and other compounds in significantly higher concentrations than are normal in surface water supplies. Among the problems that these impurities can cause is their influence on microbial growth and fouling in industrial plant water systems and heat exchangers, including cooling towers.
Managing microbial fouling is a challenging task even without the influence of the food and nutrients in POTW effluent. Accordingly, for industrial plants using these raw makeup supplies, some form of tertiary treatment is needed to make the water acceptable for raw makeup. Two technologies, among others, that have emerged in recent years to remove organics and nutrients are membrane bioreactors (MBR) and moving bed bioreactors (MBBR). For this author, who has direct experience with a large industrial activated sludge treatment system and who has also observed trickling filters, MBR and MBBR are advanced versions of those technologies. But one does not just flip a switch and walk away. Proper attention is necessary to ensure reliable operation.
Water conservation and recovery of plant wastewater discharge have been on the increase for years. The ultimate scenario is zero liquid discharge (ZLD), which reclaims essentially all non-biological waste water for reuse. Space limitations prevent a detailed discussion of ZLD methods, but a key component of many systems is reverse osmosis (RO).
In roughly the final three decades of the last century, RO became quite popular in the power industry as a retrofit ahead of existing makeup treatment ion exchange (IX) demineralizers to improve IX performance and reduce regeneration frequency. RO is now a standard component of makeup water treatment systems for many combined cycle power plants. In these applications, RO units typically convert 75% of the influent to purified water (permeate), with the remaining 25% as a reject stream. For waste stream recovery and ZLD applications, high recovery RO (with a 90:10 permeate-to-reject ratio or perhaps even greater) serves as a core concentrating mechanism. The high recovery greatly reduces the volume of the remaining stream, which in turn reduces equipment and operating costs for final processes such as evaporation/crystallization. An important note, as this author knows from direct experience, stringent RO pre- and process treatment are necessary to minimize membrane fouling and scaling.
Another much-discussed discharge control issue, albeit on the flue gas side, is carbon capture and sequestration (CCS). Of course, CCS is touted as a method for continued fossil fuel use with minimized CO2 emissions, but issues remain formidable. Similar to flue gas desulfurization for coal plants, a carbon capture system can be thought of as a chemical plant attached to the backend of the fossil-fired power plant. Parasitic power usage to scrub, regenerate and compress CO2 is of concern and is a continued driver for improved capture methods. Sequestering the concentrated compound is another issue. Some facilities sit over underground formations that can hold CO2, at least according to geological analyses, but many projects call for CO2 pipelines to move the chemical elsewhere. These pipelines are controversial, and in some instances have been successfully opposed by citizens residing along the proposed pathways. CCS will be a regular discussion point for future POWERGEN conferences.
Some Additional POWERGEN Topics
The discussion above outlined several important topics that could be a part of next year’s EUCCW (and some in main POWERGEN sessions), and the author and his colleagues would be more than glad to talk about these issues and others during the numerous networking opportunities that are part of the EUCCW. However, many other power generation topics are on the POWERGEN technical agenda and at special sessions on the exhibit floor. A comprehensive outline may be found on the POWERGEN website.
Let’s consider several items. First, renewables development is not at a complete standstill, and an eventual change in presidential administration could re-energize this industry. Certainly, wind and solar generation will remain key issues at POWERGEN. A key component of renewables growth is improvement in energy storage methods. A long-time friend of mine, and a transmission and distribution expert, never tires in pointing out that a typical coal plant maintains a 30-day fuel inventory, which is way beyond current battery capacity. More work is definitely needed to enhance renewables storage capacity.
On another note, this author will be interested in seeing how biofuels (and biochemicals) technology continues to develop, apart from well-known, corn-based ethanol production. Plants/trees offer an extensive renewable resource, but issues related to land management, materials collection, transportation, and economical conversion of carbohydrates to hydrocarbons remain major R&D issues.
For all of the generation technologies mentioned above, and others, numerous non-engineering factors will influence project development. These include:
- Supply chains and equipment procurement. Lead times have significantly lengthened.
- Financing
- Permitting, siting and community engagement
- Workforce hiring
- Safety program(s) development
- Load growth planning
And, of course, another elephant in the room is the state of the electrical grid and the improvements that will be needed to bring the grid up to the standards needed for rapidly expanding electrical demand. While these are topics on the POWERGEN agenda, another primary conference is DTECH. Look for more information on the DTECH website.
Conclusion
Many critical power generation topics will be on the agenda for POWERGEN 2027 and the accompanying EUCCW. The power industry is facing many challenges, which makes knowledge acquisition extremely important. Having worked directly in the energy sector for nearly two decades and supporting power projects for many additional years at engineering firms, I can say with confidence that POWERGEN 2027 will provide a wealth of informative details on critical power topics.
